## 02-14-2026

One of the biggest focuses of my CS studies has been understanding what it means to really *learn*. Throughout most of my formal education, I relied on rote memorization to pass tests and that was often enough to be "successful" in the education system. Most subjects didn't really apeal to me on a deep level, so there wasn't any need for me to spend more time on them than was necessary to get good grades.

I started to notice that level of effort wasn't really cutting it around my later years in university taking upper division human biology courses. For one, there was a vast amount of information to take in, and secondly, a lot of the tests went deeper than surface level concepts, and required more complex application of the knowledge. A week before these more difficult exams, I would go to the library every day and spend 4-6 hours per day going over my notes and trying to memorize as many facts as possible. Cramming was my best friend, and it had always pulled me through thick and thin.

When I took these exams after countless hours of "studying", I wondered why there would always be questions I couldn't answer well. Why did I lack this information? Where in the textbook was the answer to this? Or was it somewhere in the lecture notes? I believed I had not memorized enough, and that if I just spent a few more hours staring at the right notes, that gap would have been filled.

Leading up to my decision to pursue CS, I knew that there was a discrepancy between what I originally thought learning was, and what it really meant to learn. I would have to abandon my preconceived notions and properly *learn* how to learn.
